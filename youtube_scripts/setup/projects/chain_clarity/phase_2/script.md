## SECTION 2 — PROOF OF WORK: HOW BITCOIN IS SECURED (2:30–5:00)

[TITLE CARD: "02 · HOW NEW BLOCKS GET ADDED"]
[INFOGRAPHIC CUE: card_01 — PoW vs PoS comparison]

SPOKEN:
"So who actually adds new blocks to the Bitcoin chain?
Specialized computers called miners.

Here is how it works.

Every 10 minutes, miners compete to solve a puzzle.
The puzzle: find a number — called a nonce — such that, when you combine it with the block's data and hash the result, the output starts with a specific number of zeros.

There is no shortcut. There is no formula.
You can only find it by trying billions of random guesses per second.
The first miner to find a valid solution broadcasts the new block.
Every other node verifies it instantly.
The winner gets a reward: new Bitcoin.

This mechanism is called Proof of Work.
Why work? Because the computation costs real energy.
That energy cost is the security model.

Think of it like this.
Imagine a combination lock with a billion possible combinations.
The only way to open it is to try every number.
That takes time. That takes resources.
Anyone who wants to attack the network must do more computation than all honest miners combined.
Today, that would cost millions of dollars per hour.
That is the anchor.

Bitcoin's network currently uses roughly 150 terawatt-hours of electricity per year.
That sounds enormous. And it is.
But it also means an attacker must match that entire energy budget — sustainably — to have any chance."

## SECTION 3 — BITCOIN'S FIXED SUPPLY (5:00–7:30)

[TITLE CARD: "03 · THE 21 MILLION HARD CAP"]
[INFOGRAPHIC CUE: card_02 — halving timeline]

SPOKEN:
"Let's talk about where Bitcoin comes from.

When a miner wins the puzzle and adds a block, they receive a reward.
New Bitcoin, created by the protocol.
This is the only way new Bitcoin enters circulation.

In 2009, the reward was 50 BTC per block.
Every 210,000 blocks — roughly every four years — that reward is cut in half.
This is called a halving.

2009: 50 BTC per block.
2012: 25 BTC.
2016: 12.5 BTC.
2020: 6.25 BTC.
2024: 3.125 BTC.

The halvings continue until the reward reaches a number so small it rounds to zero.
That happens around the year 2140.
At that point, no new Bitcoin will ever be created.
Total supply: 21 million. Final.

As of 2024, about 19.7 million Bitcoin have already been mined.
Only 1.3 million remain.

Think of it like a gold mine where the vein gets thinner by half every four years.
By design. Built into the bedrock.
No negotiation. No exception.

This predictable scarcity is one of the reasons Bitcoin is often compared to gold.
But unlike gold, you can verify the total supply of Bitcoin in seconds.
Run a node. Count the coins. It is transparent and auditable by anyone."

## SECTION 4 — WHAT ETHEREUM ADDED (7:30–9:30)

[TITLE CARD: "04 · ETHEREUM: A BLOCKCHAIN WITH A BRAIN"]
[INFOGRAPHIC CUE: card_03 — Bitcoin = money vs Ethereum = computer]

SPOKEN:
"Bitcoin answered one question: how do we move value without a bank?

In 2013, a 19-year-old named Vitalik Buterin asked a different question.
What if we could run code without a server?

His insight: a blockchain is not a ledger.
It is a shared, unstoppable computer.

Bitcoin's blockchain can record: 'Alex sent 1 BTC to Maria.'
Ethereum's blockchain can record and execute: 'IF Maria deposits 100 ETH, THEN release the loan. IF she misses a payment, THEN liquidate automatically.'

This is the fundamental difference.
Bitcoin: a currency system.
Ethereum: a programmable platform.

The programs that run on Ethereum are called smart contracts.
They live on the blockchain. They run automatically.
No company behind them. No server to switch off.

Think of it this way.
Bitcoin is a vending machine.
You put money in. You get something out. That is all it does.
Ethereum is a general-purpose computer.
You can run any program on it — lending, trading, voting, identity — as long as you can write the logic.

Vitalik launched Ethereum in 2015.
Today, tens of thousands of applications run on it.
None of them require permission from Ethereum to exist."

## SECTION 5 — PROOF OF STAKE (9:30–11:30)

[TITLE CARD: "05 · ETHEREUM'S PROOF OF STAKE"]
[INFOGRAPHIC CUE: card_01 — PoW vs PoS right column]

SPOKEN:
"In September 2022, Ethereum did something Bitcoin has never done.
It changed its consensus mechanism.
This event was called The Merge.

Ethereum switched from Proof of Work to Proof of Stake.

In Proof of Work, miners compete using energy.
In Proof of Stake, validators compete using collateral.

Here is how Proof of Stake works.

To become a validator, you lock up 32 ETH as a deposit.
This is called staking.
The protocol randomly selects validators to propose new blocks.
If you propose valid blocks honestly — you earn rewards.
If you cheat, try to include invalid transactions, or go offline too often — part of your deposit is destroyed.
This is called slashing.

Your locked ETH is the security.
Cheating means losing money directly.
That financial stake replaces the energy cost.

The result: Ethereum's energy use dropped 99.9% after The Merge.
The same security guarantees — enforced by economic punishment instead of computation.

Think of it this way.
Proof of Work: prove you did the work by showing the energy bill and the blisters.
Proof of Stake: prove your commitment by putting your own money as collateral first."

## SECTION 6 — WALLETS AND KEYS (11:30–13:15)

[TITLE CARD: "06 · WALLETS, KEYS & TRANSACTIONS"]
[B-ROLL: Hardware wallet, phone wallet app]

SPOKEN:
"One of the most common misconceptions: a crypto wallet holds your Bitcoin.
It does not.

Your Bitcoin lives on the blockchain.
What a wallet holds is the key that proves you own it.

Here is the three-layer structure.

Private key.
A 256-bit number. Randomly generated. Extremely long.
This is your proof of ownership.
Whoever holds the private key controls the coins.
Lose it — the coins are inaccessible. Forever.
Share it — the coins are gone.

Public key.
Mathematically derived from the private key.
This derivation is one-way: you can generate the public key from the private key.
You cannot go backwards.

Address.
A shorter version derived from the public key.
This is what you share when someone wants to send you Bitcoin.
Like a mailbox number.

When you send Bitcoin:
You create a transaction — from address A, to address B, amount X.
You sign it with your private key.
The signature proves ownership without revealing the private key.
The transaction is broadcast to the network.
Nodes verify the signature.
If valid, it is included in the next block.

The analogy:
Your address is your mailbox. Anyone can find it. Anyone can drop something in.
Your private key is a master seal stamp. Only you can authorize an outgoing package.
The signature on the package proves it came from you — without exposing the stamp itself."
