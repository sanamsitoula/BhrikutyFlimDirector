Scarcity shapes all individual and collective actions.

**NARRATION:**
Supply and demand are core market forces.
Supply represents how much sellers offer.
Demand shows how much buyers want to purchase.
Think about your local farmers' market on a Saturday.
Many farmers arrive with fresh, ripe tomatoes.
This total amount of tomatoes is the market's supply.
Many shoppers also visit, wanting to buy tomatoes.
Their combined desire for tomatoes is the market's demand.
If many farmers bring tomatoes, supply is high.
If few people want tomatoes, demand is low.
When supply exceeds demand, prices tend to fall.
Farmers might lower prices to sell their produce.
Conversely, imagine a sudden tomato shortage.
Perhaps bad weather damaged many crops this year.
Now, fewer tomatoes are available; supply is low.
If many people still want tomatoes, demand remains high.
When demand exceeds supply, prices usually rise.
Shoppers might pay more to get their desired tomatoes.
The price eventually settles at an equilibrium point.
At this point, supply matches demand perfectly.
All farmers can sell their tomatoes.
All interested shoppers can buy them.
This interplay of supply and demand shapes all prices.
It affects your coffee, your eggs, and everything else.
It is an invisible hand guiding market transactions.

**NARRATION:**
Every decision you make has a hidden cost.
This cost is the value of your next best choice.
Economists call this the opportunity cost.
It is what you give up by choosing something else.
Imagine you decide to watch a favorite show tonight.
Your alternative might have been to exercise.
The health benefits from exercising are your opportunity cost.
You sacrificed those benefits for TV entertainment.
It's not about money, but about lost potential.
Let's consider another everyday scenario.
You choose to buy a new pair of stylish shoes.
Perhaps you could have saved that money instead.
Saving could have contributed to your future travel fund.
The lost travel fund contribution is your opportunity cost.
Businesses face these crucial trade-offs too.
A company decides to invest in new production machinery.
It must give up investing in employee training programs.
The potential benefits from training are their opportunity cost.
Governments weigh opportunity costs when making policies.
Building a new hospital means not building a new park.
The community benefits of the park are sacrificed.
Understanding opportunity cost improves your decisions.
It helps you recognize the full implications of choices.
This concept applies to every aspect of your life.
It shows that "free" choices rarely exist in reality.

**NARRATION:**
How much does a price change affect your buying?
Elasticity measures this responsiveness in economics.
Some products are very sensitive to price shifts.
Their demand is called "elastic," like vacation travel.
If airfares increase, many people postpone trips.
They seek cheaper alternatives or stay home instead.
Other products are less sensitive to price changes.
Their demand is called "inelastic," like basic food.
You still buy groceries even if prices rise slightly.
People need food to live, regardless of cost.
Consider gasoline prices at the pump.
When gas prices climb, you might drive less.
You might combine errands or choose public transport.
Your demand for gas is somewhat elastic in this case.
But you still need some gas for daily commuting.
There is a limit to how much you can reduce usage.
A dramatic price increase might change your car choice.
Companies analyze elasticity for their pricing strategies.
If demand is elastic, they might lower prices.
This can attract many more customers and boost sales.
If demand is inelastic, they can raise prices.
Customers will likely still buy, accepting the higher cost.
Understanding elasticity helps explain market behavior.
It shows us why some product prices fluctuate more.
It applies to everything from luxury goods to daily necessities.
